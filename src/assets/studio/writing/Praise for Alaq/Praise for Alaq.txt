Imagine a moving film. Slipping across screens. Photographs coming to life and then smudged out. The story shifting through space. The text lingering in time. Like a dream, with an incoherent coherence. Like a poem without rhythm. A film without a script. Nay, a film escaping the script and dancing with thin air. A story as real as flesh and mortality, but the storytelling just an ephemeral shifting of lights sounds and voice.

I struggle to find the words to describe, first, the very motions of the immersive film by Pallavi Paul at the Kochi Muziris Biennale, and, second, the evocative effect it has. Through a delicate interplay of visuals, audio, and sound, it tells the story of love and loss through a nipah outbreak. The three screen set up is simply the stage upon which she choregaphs this dance of visuals shifting across screens; the sound situates itself in whichever corner it needs to, and the subtitles become a main character as they follow their own rules through space and time. The storytelling stubbornly refuses to be reined in by descriptive words. Parts of a letter appear one word at a time; photographs come to life; three screens show three different views of the same moment; phrases are selectively dropped from a text; the same photo fades into itself only, a different field of view this time. These are only some of the poetic visual tools that Pallavi Paul engages to create a simultaneous sense of permanence and ephemerality, lending a dreamlike quality to a story about flesh and death.

While the storytelling itself manages to evoke sharp and tender emotions, the story hugs you, unsettles you, carves your heart out, and teaches you to stitch it back in again. It remains a poignant telling of the absurdity and the surreal quality of seeing your loved one dying without realising they are dying. The ungrand love that can fill up a person's world. The loss that can shatter it. And the little bits of thread that tie it together again.
